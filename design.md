What Actually Happens to One USDG Deposit, Start to Finish

Your USDG, the dollar stablecoin this vault is built around, is asleep somewhere, in a lending pool that looked reasonable on the day you chose it. That is the shape of stablecoin yield today: plentiful, public and badly distributed. The best rate available at any hour is a matter of record. Almost nobody earns it.

The reasons are mechanical. A lending pool's supply rate is a function of utilization, the share of supplied capital that borrowers have taken out. When capital arrives, utilization falls and the rate falls with it, so a pool paying twelve percent this week pays four the next. Your own deposit does the same thing to you: a pool advertising twelve percent on two million of liquidity does not still pay twelve percent after another million arrives, so the rate worth chasing is the one left after your capital lands, not the headline, and only splitting across venues keeps each position on the steeper part of its own curve. Watching a dozen pools and moving between them is a full-time job nobody signed up for, and every move costs more than it looks: gas (the network's per-transaction fee) on both legs, plus execution risk, the chance they do not land at the prices you planned on; across five positions it is five times the job and five times the gas. A strategy that reshuffles on every rate twitch destroys more value than it captures; this is how sophisticated strategies underperform lazy ones. So people park in one venue and stay.

The cost of staying is collected quietly. As an illustration only, the same one used in the essay "Your Money Is Asleep", the gap between four percent and nine percent on $50,000 is $2,500 in a year and more than $8,000 compounded over three. Mosaic Capital is built to take the job off the depositor without taking away visibility. What follows tracks one deposit through the machine, saying at each step what the code enforces and what is still a promise.

You are depositing into an ERC-4626 vault (the standard interface for tokenized vaults) over USDG. The shares are mUSDG, a standard token (ERC-20, the format every wallet already understands) that also carries the ERC-4626 vault interface, so any ERC-4626-aware wallet can read, price and transfer it, and your share count never changes on its own. Yield arrives in exactly one form, the price per share rising. There is no claim button anywhere in Mosaic and no reward token; the shares you hold on day one are the shares you hold in month six, worth more USDG.

First, accounting. mUSDG shares carry twelve decimals, USDG's six plus a six-decimal offset. The offset keeps the rounding loss on a deposit-and-withdraw round trip to about a millionth of a USDG, and it makes the classic ERC-4626 inflation attack (an attacker donating tokens to an empty vault to distort the share price and pocket the next depositor's rounding) uneconomic: the donation would have to be a million times larger than the value at stake. Total assets are the idle buffer (USDG the vault holds directly) plus what every adapter (a small contract, one per venue, through which the vault talks to it) reports it holds, principal plus accrued interest. Price per share is total assets divided by total shares, scaled to start at exactly one; after your first deposit at par, everything is that number moving.

Your USDG does not go to a lending venue on its own. It waits in the idle buffer, which targets six percent of total assets, and the keeper, the operator's automation script, deploys what sits above that target in a batch split pro rata to the target weights, each slice clamped by that venue's room and cap (anything clamped stays idle), so the keeper pays the gas of entering each venue once per batch and a small depositor gets a large depositor's economics. Until the next batch lands your USDG sits idle, pulling the blended rate down slightly for everyone, though your shares already own a slice of everything deployed. The buffer's second job is paying ordinary withdrawals without touching a venue, so the exit costs nothing beyond the transaction.

The vault does not know what a venue is; the core is deliberately small and does not change, and every new venue is a new adapter, never an edit to the vault. Turret, a lending protocol on Robinhood Chain, is the first venue it is built to reach. Every adapter speaks a small fixed vocabulary called IPoolAdapter, eight functions in all. Four move or count money: deposit, withdraw (returning what was actually received), totalAssets (principal plus interest) and currentRateBps, the annual rate in basis points (hundredths of a percent). One, asset, names the token and is checked at registration. Three price liquidity and risk on-chain instead of assuming them: availableLiquidity (free cash the adapter can reach), maxDeposit (room left) and utilizationBps (borrowed over supplied).

Three parties can act on your money once it is deployed. The owner is intended to be a multisig, a wallet several named people must approve. It can register and remove adapters, set target weights and every parameter (most within a coded bound: the fee at 30 percent, basis-point values at 100 percent; cooldown, horizon, deposit cap, per-adapter asset cap and gas-quote ceiling are unbounded), pause, and emergency-withdraw any one adapter back to idle. No function lets it move depositor assets to itself directly; its path to your money runs through the registry: an owner that registers a hostile adapter and points the weights at it can lose the book, which is what the multisig is for.

The keeper acts through a hot key, a signing key kept on an internet-connected server so a script can use it unattended. Its authority is timing, not direction, and it is bounded on every side. It chooses when the vault deploys, samples rates or rebalances and, once the owner turns scoring on, when the on-chain score becomes the target weights. It cannot send funds anywhere except registered adapters. It cannot exceed a per-adapter weight ceiling; the contract checks every weight written. It cannot skip the drift threshold or the 24-hour cooldown. It cannot price rebalancing out of existence once the owner has capped its gas quote (the cap starts unbounded). It never holds vault assets.

An adapter is trusted code: a malicious one can steal what was deployed to it. Registration is owner-only, and every adapter starts with a weight ceiling of 40 percent, which the owner can raise as far as 100 percent: a starting point, not a hard bound. The contract enforces that no target weight, manual or scored, exceeds the ceiling when written; lowering a ceiling afterwards does not shrink an existing target until weights are set again. The numbers an adapter reports are not trusted: any rate is clamped at 100,000 basis points (a 1,000 percent annual yield) before it touches arithmetic, and a liquidity claim can only shrink what the vault attempts, never expand it.

The scoring engine chooses where capital goes. An adapter's score is its rate, times its liquidity, times a utilization discount that halves the score at full utilization, times a volatility discount capped at a halving. The rate term is the smoothed supply rate once a sample exists, the clamped spot rate before that. The liquidity term is free cash over held balance, so a venue that cannot pay out scores zero. The volatility term subtracts the rate's average recent swing (its smoothed mean absolute deviation) relative to the rate itself, capped at 5,000 basis points. The smoothed rate is an exponential moving average weighting each new sample at 20 percent, sampled whenever the keeper deploys, rebalances or applies scored weights, and on demand through pokeRates, which builds history before any of those run so the first rebalance plans on a smoothed rate rather than one spot reading.

Scores become weights by being normalized to 10,000 basis points and then capped: any adapter whose share would reach or exceed its ceiling is pinned there and the remainder redistributed among those with room. Rounding leaves a few basis points unassigned. That dust goes only onto adapters that scored, never onto one the scorer rejected and never past a ceiling; if it cannot be placed, the call reverts (the chain rejects it and undoes its changes). The same refusal applies when the scored adapters' ceilings cannot cover the whole book: the existing targets stay in force and the keeper logs a skip. Scoring is opt-in: off by default, with target weights set by the owner; once enabled, the keeper may apply scored weights.

Rates move and the book drifts from its targets. A rebalance must clear four gates (plus the obvious refusals: targets unset, nothing to move, vault paused), and if any one fails the vault does nothing. The first is a cooldown of 24 hours since the last successful rebalance. The second is drift: the largest gap between any adapter's current weight and its target must exceed 0.5 percent. The third is economics. The vault plans pulls from over-weight adapters, each capped at that venue's free cash, and pushes to under-weight ones bounded by what the pulls can fund, prices the plan over a 30-day horizon (each push times its destination's smoothed rate, minus each pull times its source's), subtracts the assumed gas cost (a USDG figure the keeper quotes) and requires the result to clear a floor of minus 0.5 percent of the assets moved. The floor exists because restoring target weights is a risk decision, not a yield decision; at zero, every rebalance must pay for itself net of gas. Because a hot key writes a number the gate depends on, the owner can cap the quote; anything above the cap reverts, and in code the cap starts unbounded.

The fourth gate is execution. Each unwind must land within 0.1 percent of what was requested (the shortfall is called slippage) or the whole transaction reverts, and total assets before and after are compared against the same bound, so the round trip cannot leak more than that. Pushes are bounded by what was actually pulled and by each destination's cap and room; anything unplaced stays in the idle buffer. A read-only function called previewRebalance, free for anyone to call, returns whether a rebalance would go and the numbers behind that answer. The dashboard and the keeper read those same numbers, though the preview uses stored smoothed rates and the real rebalance samples fresh ones, so near the threshold they can differ by a hair.

Meanwhile the vault takes its fee: 10 percent of yield and nothing else, hard-capped in code at 30 percent. It is measured against a high-water mark, the highest price per share the vault has recorded, so if the vault does not earn there is no fee, and after a drawdown none applies until the previous peak is passed. It is minted as mUSDG shares to the fee recipient rather than withdrawn as USDG, so it dilutes the price per share rather than removing assets. It is accrued before every deposit, mint, withdraw and redeem, so nobody pays for a gain they did not receive. It is never charged on principal, and one edge case shows the care that takes: a gain so small that 10 percent of it rounds to zero shares. A naive implementation would skip the fee and leave the mark where it was, so the same gain would be charged the next time the price rose enough to mint a whole share, which is a fee on principal by another name. Mosaic's code moves the mark up even when it mints nothing, so a gain declined once is never counted again.

Eventually you want out. A withdrawal is one transaction: shares in, USDG out at the current price per share. The vault pays from the idle buffer first and, if that is short, unwinds adapters in registry order until the amount is covered. There are no partial fills: the withdrawal completes in full or reverts with your shares untouched. The figure the dashboard shows is withdrawalCapacity: the buffer plus the cash each venue could pay now, with balances lent to borrowers excluded until repaid. maxWithdraw and maxRedeem, the two standard functions a wallet consults, are bounded by it. It does not promise an instant full exit in all conditions; it does promise withdrawals never wait on the keeper.

The deposit cap is 2,000,000 USDG by default, and maxDeposit reports zero once it is reached and whenever the vault is paused. Pause blocks deposits, minting, deployment and rebalancing; withdrawals, redemptions and the emergency pull continue, so the owner can stop money coming in and moving but not leaving.

Anyone describing a yield product without naming its failure modes is describing a brochure, so here they are. A venue can fail; that is why no adapter may be assigned more than its ceiling, 40 percent of the book by default (a bound on what the vault will send there, not on what it may hold at any instant, since withdrawals and interest can push a venue above it between rebalances), and why the owner can pull any one adapter back to idle in one call. Containment has an order, because deploy follows target weights, not ceilings, and the keeper's next tick would otherwise push the recovered USDG straight back into the pulled venue. Pause is the reliable first step; it stops deployment and rebalancing while the emergency pull still works. Zeroing the adapter's ceiling only stops new weight being written to it: the target it already carries persists, and with three adapters at the default 40 percent ceiling the two that remain cover only 80 percent of the book, so scored weights revert and manual weights cannot sum to 100 percent until another ceiling is raised. So: pause, pull, zero the adapter's ceiling, raise the others to cover the book, re-set the weights (by hand, or by the keeper's next applyScoredWeights if scoring is on), unpause, and remove the adapter once it reports an empty balance and carries no target weight, since removal reverts on either condition. The vault's own contracts are a target; that is why the core is small and adapters can be removed alone. The allocation model can misjudge; the ceilings bound how much a bad reading can attract, the cooldown stops it churning the book, the slippage bound caps what one rebalance can lose in execution, and the economics gate blocks moves its own estimates say are not worth it.

The keeper can stall or be compromised; stalled, it degrades yield but cannot lose funds; compromised, it is bounded as described above. And venues cost dust: when the vault redeems part of a position, the pool rounds in its own favor. In the test suite's mock pools (stand-in contracts that imitate a lending pool) that costs up to a millionth of a USDG per venue touched. A withdrawal-path unwind is not slippage-bounded the way a rebalance is; a venue paying out less than asked has the shortfall covered by the next adapter and borne by remaining holders. So the share price can tick down by a hair.

The contracts carry 63 tests across three suites: unit tests for the main paths; fuzz tests, 256 random input sets each, against deposit-and-withdraw round trips and the scoring engine; and seven invariants, statements that must stay true whatever happens, checked through a simulation of three depositors, a keeper, moving rates, borrowers draining pools and time passing, at the default of Foundry, the toolkit the contracts are built with: 256 runs of 500 calls, 128,000 per invariant. The seven cover the ledger adding up, the high-water mark never falling while shares exist, assets leaving only through withdrawals apart from dust bounded at 0.01 USDG per run, capacity never exceeding holdings, shares staying fully backed, targets summing to exactly 10,000 or to zero, and principal never being underwater beyond the same dust allowance.

Fuzzing found two defects in the scoring engine that reading did not, and a third was fixed in the fee math: a performance fee that could touch principal, because when the fee rounded to zero shares the high-water mark did not move and the same gain was charged later. The scoring defects were rounding dust that could fund an adapter the scorer had rejected, and a score divided back down after each multiplication, so a venue paying a few basis points floored to zero and dropped out of the book. The scoring fixes are covered by tests; the fee fix is not: nothing in the suite exercises the zero-share case, and the high-water-mark invariant only checks that the mark never falls, which the original bug also satisfied, so a regression would pass undetected. Not verified: there has been no third-party audit, and no formal verification of the fee math.

The keeper is a script that polls the chain and runs the same sequence every tick. It calls pokeRates if the oldest sample is stale. It applies scored weights only if the operator switched that on and the owner enabled scoring on-chain. It deploys anything above the buffer. It refreshes its gas quote when it has a native-token price to convert with; without one the on-chain quote stays at its default of zero and the economics gate is weaker. Finally it reads previewRebalance and rebalances only when every gate clears. It simulates every call before sending and logs a skip when the contract would reject it. It fails over between RPC endpoints (the servers it reads and writes the chain through), times out a wedged tick rather than the process, deduplicates alerts and exposes a health endpoint when configured to. A dry-run mode requires no key and stops every call after simulation, so the loop can be exercised against a real chain for free. Two or more instances are safe to run at once for the same reason: the loser of the race normally sees the vault reject the call at simulation (cooldown active, nothing to deploy) and sends nothing; only if both simulate before either transaction lands does the loser pay for a reverted transaction, which is what the jitter (a small random delay before each tick) and a distinct name per instance are for.

Nothing that happens to your deposit is hidden. The dashboard reads total value locked, the share price, every venue's allocation against its target, withdrawal capacity, drift and rebalance economics, and a history of every deployment, rebalance, fee accrual and target change read straight from the contract's event logs in the browser, with no indexer and no server in between. Today that is a local test chain with mock pools, so the numbers are test numbers. The site's figures are read the same way and show a dash when the chain cannot be reached.

The Turret adapter has not been written, because Turret's pool interface is not yet public; the adapters that exist today are mocks, and none of them touches a real venue. The production chain, RPC, explorer, USDG and pool addresses are still to be filled in. The keeper key is still a single hot key; the failover, timeouts, alerts and health checks make its failures loud, not impossible. Every item on the pre-deployment checklist is still open.

What comes next is depth before choice: more adapters first, so the allocation logic can be tuned against real execution data rather than simulation; then a conservative vault touching only the most established venues and an aggressive one reaching further, the same machinery under different ceilings; after that, integrations that let other protocols accept mUSDG as collateral. None of these exist today.

Here is where that leaves a depositor: the share accounting, the fee cap, the weight ceilings, the cooldown, the profitability floor, the slippage bounds and the all-or-nothing withdrawal are enforced by a contract you can read. The choice of venues, the ceilings themselves, the hot key, the audit and the production deployment are still in the operator's hands. What is left for you is one deposit, many sources of yield and zero monitoring: the share price carries the result, and we judge this product by how rarely you need to open it. The name is the design: a mosaic is small fragments arranged until they read as one image. Many fragments. One picture.
