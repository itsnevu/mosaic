There is a strange irony at the center of stablecoins today. The yield is there, plenty of it, scattered across dozens of lending pools at rates that make a bank savings account look like a joke told with a straight face. So ask yourself honestly: when was the last time you actually earned the best yield available to you? Probably never. Not because you are lazy, but because chasing it is a full time job nobody signed up for, and the people who do it well are either running a desk or running a bot.

Look at what actually happens. Rates move constantly. A pool paying twelve percent this week drops to four the next, because liquidity floods in the moment it looks good, and nobody sends you a notification when it happens. There are a dozen pools worth watching, each with its own dashboard, its own risk profile, its own quirks. Every time you move, you pay gas, you sign an approval, you hope you did not misread a line. And spreading across five pools instead of one, which is what prudence actually demands, means five times the work and five times the cost. So here is what people really do. They put their USDG somewhere that looks safe enough and leave it there for months. The capital sits still. The yield goes to whoever had the time.

The gap looks small on paper. Four percent versus nine percent sounds like something you can shrug at. On fifty thousand dollars that is twenty five hundred in a single year, and compounded over three years it is more than eight thousand. On serious capital across a full cycle it is the difference between a position that grew and a position that merely survived. That inefficiency is not a market bug. It is a tax paid by ordinary people for the crime of having other things to do, and it is collected quietly, in increments too small to notice on any given day.

Understanding why the gap persists matters, because the obvious fixes make it worse. The naive answer is to sort pools by advertised rate and put everything in the highest one. That fails twice over. A headline rate says nothing about whether it survives your arrival, and it says nothing about what happens if that pool has a bad day. The highest yields almost always arrive holding hands with the highest risk, and concentration turns a survivable event into a total loss. The subtler answer, rebalancing manually across several pools, is correct in principle and impossible in practice, because the cost of each move exceeds its visible benefit on any single occasion while the cost of skipping it compounds invisibly. Mosaic Capital exists to remove that trade off entirely.

The name is not decorative. A mosaic is a pile of small fragments that becomes a single image once someone arranges them properly. That is exactly how we see yield: not as one pool you have to guess correctly, but as dozens of fragments that need assembling into a portfolio, and you should not have to assemble them yourself. You deposit USDG once. That is your entire job. After that the protocol works. Your money is not thrown at a single best pool. It is allocated across many lending pools at once, Turret among them, weighted by what each one is actually paying and what each one actually risks. You are not betting on a horse. You are holding the field.

Here is the part almost nobody explains, and it is the reason spreading beats concentrating as arithmetic rather than as caution. Yield in a lending market is a function of utilization, and supplying capital raises the supply side, which lowers utilization, which lowers the rate. A pool advertising twelve percent with two million of liquidity will not still be paying twelve percent after you add a million. The engine therefore optimizes for the rate the vault will actually receive after the allocation exists, not the rate that was on the screen before. Yield curves flatten as you push into them, so splitting across several pools lets each position sit on a steeper, more productive part of its own curve. Diversification here is the higher return, not the price you pay for safety.

Knowing the ideal allocation is not the same as moving to it, and this is where most automated strategies quietly bleed. Rebalancing costs gas and carries execution risk, so a vault that reshuffles itself every time a rate twitches burns more value than it captures. Overtrading is how sophisticated strategies underperform lazy ones. A rebalance therefore fires only when the expected additional yield, over a realistic holding period, exceeds the full cost of the move. Drift under half a percent is allowed to sit. A twenty four hour cooldown stops the vault oscillating between two pools that keep trading places. A keeper can trigger the move but cannot invent instructions: it cannot send funds anywhere except a registered adapter, cannot exceed the weight ceilings, and cannot bypass the cooldown. Its freedom is limited to when, never to where.

Two more mechanisms are worth naming because they decide how the product actually feels. The first is that there is no claim button anywhere in Mosaic, ever. Yield does not arrive as a separate token you must remember to harvest. It arrives as the price per share rising, so you hold the same shares in month six that you held on day one and they are simply worth more USDG. The vault follows the ERC-4626 standard, which means any wallet or protocol can read your position without custom code. The second is that deposits accumulate in an idle buffer and deploy in batches rather than moving on chain individually, so the gas cost of entering a pool is shared across everyone in that batch instead of shouldered alone. A small depositor gets a large depositor's economics. That buffer has a second job too: ordinary withdrawals are served straight from it, instantly, without unwinding any position.

The guardrails around all of this are constraints in a contract rather than promises in a document, which is a different kind of assurance. No single pool may exceed forty percent of the vault regardless of how attractive it looks. Every adapter passes an audit and an observation period at limited size before going live. A deposit cap holds while the system proves itself. The performance fee is ten percent, charged on yield only and tracked against a high water mark, so if the vault does not earn, the fee does not apply and nobody at Mosaic gets paid for your money sitting still. It is never charged on principal, and that is enforced in code. And nothing is hidden: the dashboard shows the yield you are actually earning derived from your own share price net of fees, exactly which pool holds which portion of your deposit, current weight against target, and the full history of every reallocation with what it cost. You are free to never look, but the moment you want to, there is nothing concealed. Anyone describing a yield product without naming its failure modes is describing a brochure, so here are the three real ones: a lending pool you are supplied to could fail, which is why allocation is spread and capped rather than concentrated; Mosaic's own contracts are a target, which is why the core is deliberately small and unchanging while complexity lives in adapters that can be audited alone and disabled individually; and the allocation model itself could misjudge, which is why weight ceilings, cooldowns and profitability checks exist to make a bad decision survivable rather than catastrophic.

What comes out of this is three things and no promised number. Yield closer to the top of the market than the bottom, measured against the only benchmark that matters, which is whether a protocol managed portfolio consistently beats parking your money in one pool and walking away, after fees and after gas. Risk spread thin instead of stacked, so a single pool going wrong scratches your position rather than erasing it, and the diversification usually reserved for large capital becomes the default for a deposit of any size. And your time back, which is the hardest to measure and the most valuable to have, because we judge this product not by how often you open it but by how rarely you need to. The USDG vault is the first fragment of a larger picture. Next comes depth: more adapters so the field keeps widening, sharper allocation logic tuned against real execution data, and keeper run rebalancing that happens on schedule rather than when someone remembers. Then comes choice, with a conservative vault that touches only the most established pools and an aggressive one that reaches for sharper opportunities, the same machinery under different risk ceilings. After that the vault share itself becomes useful, because a standard compliant token representing a diversified yield position is something other protocols can accept as collateral, so your capital can be earning in a dozen pools and still be doing something else somewhere. And the long ambition is to become the default layer for idle stablecoins, so that one day the question where do I put my USDG has a boring, obvious answer, the same way people stopped wondering where to keep cash once a savings account became an unremarkable thing to have. Idle capital should be the anomaly, not the resting state. Many fragments. One picture. Mosaic Capital, one deposit and many sources of yield.
